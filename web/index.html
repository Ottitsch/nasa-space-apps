          How this works

          What you are looking at. NISAR, a radar satellite built by NASA and ISRO, images the same ground every 12 days. Comparing the radar phase between two passes (a technique called InSAR) shows how far the ground moved toward or away from the satellite, down to millimetres. We chain those comparisons into a history of ground motion for each place, and fit a rate. Sinking is only reported where it is clearly above the noise, on built up or bare ground, and sustained through the record.

          Measured and projected. Everything up to the latest radar pass is measured. Everything after it is a projection that assumes the ground keeps sinking at the rate measured so far. That is a scenario, not a forecast: rates change when pumping, rainfall or regulation change.

          Why buildings are coloured. A building that sinks evenly with its whole neighbourhood is usually fine structurally, though flooding and drainage get worse. Damage comes from uneven sinking, where one side of a street drops faster than the other. We measure that as angular distortion: the difference in sinking between neighbouring ground divided by the distance between them. Engineering practice uses rough limits of 1/500 (cracking becomes possible), 1/300 (cracking likely) and 1/150 (structural damage likely). Buildings change colour on the date the ground beneath them passes each limit, counted from the first radar pass. The records are still too short for any ground to have passed a limit, so the damage classes are empty at the latest radar pass and fill in along the projection. The place panel says when each limit is first passed.

          What this cannot tell you. Each radar pixel is 80 m wide, so this is a screening map of neighbourhoods, not an inspection of a single building. Distortion that built up before NISAR started watching is not included, so real conditions can be worse than shown. Places with only a few months of passes have a higher detection limit, and slow sinking there stays invisible for now. Farmland, vegetation and wetland are left blank because moisture there fools the radar. Motion is read as vertical. Building outlines are machine drawn from aerial imagery, so some are missing or misshapen, and their heights are not known (all are drawn the same height).
