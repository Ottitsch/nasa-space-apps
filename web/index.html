          Sinking Ground

          Where the land is going down, measured from orbit by NISAR.

          What you are looking at. NISAR, a radar satellite built by NASA and ISRO, images the same ground every 12 days. Comparing the radar phase between two passes (a technique called InSAR) shows how far the ground moved toward or away from the satellite, down to millimetres. We chain those comparisons into a history of ground motion for each place, and fit a rate. Sinking is only reported where it is clearly above the noise, on built up or bare ground, and sustained through the record.

          Measured and projected. Everything up to the latest radar pass is measured. Everything after it is a projection that assumes the ground keeps sinking at the rate measured so far. That is a scenario, not a forecast: rates change when pumping, rainfall or regulation change.

          Why buildings are coloured. A building that sinks evenly with its whole neighbourhood is usually fine structurally, though flooding and drainage get worse. Damage comes from uneven sinking, where one side of a street drops faster than the other. We measure that as angular distortion: the difference in sinking between neighbouring ground divided by the distance between them. Engineering practice uses rough limits of 1/500 (cracking becomes possible), 1/300 (cracking likely) and 1/150 (structural damage likely). Buildings change colour on the date the ground beneath them passes each limit.

          Sinking from before NISAR. The radar has only watched since late 2025. That is far too short for any ground to pass a limit, while the ground it sees sinking was sinking long before. So the map also counts the years before it, by assuming the sinking and the uneven sinking measured now were already going on at the same rate since 2000. That applies to how far the ground has sunk (the blue layer, the bubbles, the big number) and to the damage classes. It is the assumption the projection makes, applied backwards, and it is an estimate, not a measurement: the place panel always says how much of the total NISAR measured. Under Sinking before NISAR you can change it to 2015 or switch it off to see only what NISAR measured.

          Your address. Type an address in the search to read the ground under it. Where no place on the map covers it, the ground around it is measured on request: the radar passes over the 56 km around it are downloaded from NASA and processed the same way, which takes about 5 to 15 minutes, and the result is kept for the next person. The address is looked up with OpenStreetMap from your browser; only the nearest point of a 20 km grid is sent to this site.

          What this cannot tell you. Each radar pixel is 80 m wide, so this is a screening map of neighbourhoods, not an inspection of a single building. Distortion that built up before NISAR started watching is assumed, not measured: where sinking began after 2000 or used to be slower the damage classes overstate, and where it used to be faster they understate. Places with only a few months of passes have a higher detection limit, and slow sinking there stays invisible for now. Farmland, vegetation and wetland are left blank because moisture there fools the radar. Motion is read as vertical. Building outlines are machine drawn from aerial imagery, so some are missing or misshapen, and their heights are not known (all are drawn the same height).

          Data. NISAR Level 2 geocoded unwrapped interferograms (GUNW), NASA and ISRO, from the Alaska Satellite Facility. Elevation from the Copernicus DEM. Land cover from ESA WorldCover. Blue Marble and night lights from NASA GIBS. Close up imagery is Sentinel-2 cloudless by EOX IT Services GmbH (contains modified Copernicus Sentinel data 2024). Building outlines from Google Open Buildings, Microsoft Building Footprints and OpenStreetMap, merged by VIDA. Borders, roads and names from OpenFreeMap, OpenMapTiles and OpenStreetMap contributors.
